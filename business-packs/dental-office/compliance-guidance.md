# Dental Office — Compliance Guidance

**This is a starting point for understanding what to review, not legal advice.** Dental practices are a heavily regulated healthcare business — talk to a healthcare attorney or compliance consultant for anything specific to your state or situation.

## Data you're likely handling

- Protected Health Information (PHI): patient medical/dental history, treatment records, x-rays
- Insurance information
- Payment information

## Areas to review

1. **HIPAA compliance** — The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act sets federal rules for how patient health information must be protected, who can access it, and how breaches must be handled. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services' Office for Civil Rights (hhs.gov/hipaa) publishes the actual rules and plain-language guidance, including a summary aimed at small healthcare providers. A HIPAA risk assessment (identifying where patient data lives and who can access it) is a real, expected practice — not optional paperwork.
2. **Business Associate Agreements (BAAs)** — Any vendor or software that stores, processes, or transmits patient data on your behalf (practice management software, a texting/reminder service, a cloud backup provider) is legally required to sign a BAA with you under HIPAA. Ask any vendor directly whether they'll sign one before adopting their tool.
3. **State dental board licensing** — Every state has a dental board that licenses dentists (and often hygienists and assistants) and sets practice standards. Search "[your state] board of dentistry" or "[your state] dental board" to confirm requirements and verify licenses.
4. **Bloodborne pathogen exposure** — Dental work regularly involves exposure to blood and saliva. OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens Standard (29 CFR 1910.1030) sets requirements for safe handling, protective equipment, and exposure response plans. See osha.gov for the full standard.
5. **Infection control guidelines** — The CDC publishes specific infection control guidance for dental healthcare settings (search "CDC dental infection control guidelines" at cdc.gov), covering sterilization, instrument reprocessing, and safe practices between patients.
6. **Insurance and billing accuracy** — Errors in insurance claims (upcoding, billing for services not rendered) can create serious legal exposure beyond just a rejected claim. If claims volume is high, a dedicated insurance coordinator or billing specialist trained specifically in dental billing is a common and worthwhile investment.

## When to bring in a professional

- Before adopting any new software that will touch patient data, to confirm a BAA is in place
- If a patient complaint escalates toward a formal board complaint or legal issue
- Periodically (many practices do this annually) for a formal HIPAA risk assessment, ideally by someone independent of daily operations
