# Construction — Compliance Guidance

**This is a starting point for understanding what to review, not legal advice.** Talk to a lawyer, your state contractor licensing board, or a licensed insurance agent for anything specific to your state or situation — construction licensing, safety, and lien laws vary significantly by state.

## Data you're likely handling

- Client name, address, contact information
- Contracts, bids, and payment records
- Employee or subcontractor personal information

## Areas to review

1. **Contractor licensing** — Most states require a license to perform general contracting work, and many require separate, specific licenses for trades like electrical, plumbing, and HVAC. Requirements vary a lot by state and sometimes by city/county. Search "[your state] contractor licensing board" to confirm what's required for your specific trade and project size.
2. **Worker classification (employee vs. subcontractor)** — This is a significant area of legal risk in construction, where subcontracting is common. Misclassifying an employee as a subcontractor can create real tax, insurance, and legal exposure. The IRS (irs.gov, search "independent contractor vs employee") and the U.S. Department of Labor both publish guidance on how this distinction is made.
3. **Jobsite safety (OSHA)** — Construction has its own dedicated set of federal safety standards under 29 CFR 1926 (separate from OSHA's general industry standards), covering fall protection, scaffolding, electrical safety, and more. See osha.gov for the construction-specific standards, and consider OSHA 10 or OSHA 30 training for crew members and supervisors.
4. **Workers' compensation insurance** — Most states require workers' comp coverage for employees, and rules about whether it also applies to subcontractors vary by state. Check with your state's workers' compensation board or a licensed insurance agent.
5. **Building permits and code compliance** — Permit requirements vary by project type and local jurisdiction. Skipping a required permit can create serious legal and financial exposure if discovered later (including at resale). Check with your local building department before starting a project that may require one.
6. **Mechanic's lien rights** — Most states allow contractors and subcontractors to file a lien against a property if they aren't paid for completed work, but the process and deadlines to preserve that right are strict and vary by state. If payment disputes are a recurring issue, research your state's specific lien law requirements (often summarized by state bar associations or contractor associations) before you need to use it.

## When to bring in a professional

- Before taking on work requiring a license you don't currently hold
- If a jobsite injury occurs, to understand workers' comp and OSHA reporting obligations
- If a payment dispute is heading toward needing to file a mechanic's lien
