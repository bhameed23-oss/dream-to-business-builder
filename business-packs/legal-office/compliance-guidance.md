# Legal Office — Compliance Guidance

**This is a starting point for understanding what to review, not legal advice — including for a law firm reading about its own compliance.** State bar ethics rules are specific and vary; talk to your state bar's ethics counsel or a professional responsibility attorney for anything specific to your situation.

## Data you're likely handling

- Attorney-client privileged and confidential client information
- Client trust funds (if handling settlements, retainers, or other client money)
- Payment information

## Areas to review

1. **Rules of Professional Conduct** — Every state bar has its own Rules of Professional Conduct, most based on (but not identical to) the American Bar Association's Model Rules of Professional Conduct. These cover confidentiality, conflicts of interest, advertising, and fee agreements. Search "[your state] rules of professional conduct" or check your state bar association's website directly — this is the primary compliance document for the practice.
2. **Conflict-of-interest checking** — The Rules of Professional Conduct require checking for conflicts before accepting a new matter. A systematic process (checked against current and former clients, not just memory) matters more as a firm grows past one attorney.
3. **IOLTA / trust accounting** — If the firm holds client funds (retainers, settlement funds), state bar rules govern how those funds must be kept separate from operating funds and reconciled. Requirements and audit procedures vary by state — check your state bar's IOLTA program page directly. Trust accounting errors are one of the most common sources of bar discipline, even when unintentional.
4. **Attorney advertising rules** — Many states have specific rules about how legal services can be advertised (claims about results, use of terms like "specialist," client testimonials). Review your state bar's advertising rules before publishing marketing materials or website copy making any results-based claims.
5. **Malpractice insurance** — While not universally required, professional liability (malpractice) insurance is standard practice for attorneys in private practice. Some states do require disclosure to clients if a firm doesn't carry it — check your state bar's rules.
6. **Data security for privileged information** — While HIPAA doesn't apply to most legal practices, the duty of confidentiality under the Rules of Professional Conduct still requires reasonable safeguards for client information, especially with cloud-based practice management tools. Ask any software vendor about their security practices and data handling before adopting a new tool.

## When to bring in a professional

- Before adopting a new fee structure (especially contingency fees, which have specific rule requirements)
- If a trust accounting discrepancy is discovered, however small — address it immediately and document the correction
- If a client complaint escalates toward a bar grievance
