# Enterprise — Compliance Guidance

**This is a starting point for understanding what to review, not legal advice.** A growing business crosses several legal thresholds simply by adding headcount — this section flags the most common ones, but an employment attorney or HR compliance professional should confirm what actually applies to your specific business and state.

## Data you're likely handling

- Employee personal and payroll information across a larger workforce
- Data spread across more systems than a smaller business typically uses
- Customer/client data at greater volume and often across more employees with access

## Areas to review

1. **Employment law headcount thresholds** — Several major U.S. federal employment laws only apply once a business crosses a specific employee-count threshold, which means a growing business can suddenly become subject to a law it wasn't before, often without anyone noticing the moment it happened:
   - **Title VII of the Civil Rights Act** and the **Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)** generally apply to employers with 15 or more employees.
   - The **Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA)** generally applies to employers with 20 or more employees.
   - The **Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA)** generally applies to employers with 50 or more employees within a 75-mile radius.
   - The **Affordable Care Act's employer shared responsibility provisions** (offering health coverage) generally apply to employers with 50 or more full-time-equivalent employees.
   These are federal thresholds — many states set their own, sometimes lower, thresholds for similar protections. Check the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (eeoc.gov) and Department of Labor (dol.gov) for federal specifics, and your state labor department for state-level rules.
2. **Data access governance** — As the number of employees and systems grows, informally granted access tends to accumulate without ever being reviewed or revoked. A periodic access review (who can see what, and do they still need to) is standard practice in larger organizations and increasingly expected by clients, vendors, and cybersecurity insurance providers.
3. **Vendor and contract review at scale** — Contracts and insurance policies signed when the business was smaller may no longer reflect its actual size, revenue, or risk exposure. A periodic review with a business attorney and insurance broker helps catch coverage gaps before they matter.
4. **Board or advisory governance** — Even businesses that aren't formally required to have a board often benefit from a more structured advisory or leadership review process at this scale, to avoid every significant decision resting on one person's judgment alone.
5. **Cybersecurity and vendor security expectations** — Larger organizations, and the clients/vendors they work with, increasingly expect basic security practices (access controls, incident response plans) to be formalized rather than informal. This isn't always a legal requirement, but it's increasingly a practical business one.

## When to bring in a professional

- Before or as soon as employee count approaches any of the thresholds above — a proactive HR/legal review is far cheaper than being caught out of compliance after crossing one unnoticed
- Before a contract or insurance renewal, to confirm coverage still matches the business's actual current scale
- When formalizing a decision-rights or governance structure for the first time
