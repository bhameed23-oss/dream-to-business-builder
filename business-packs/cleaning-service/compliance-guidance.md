# Cleaning Service — Compliance Guidance

**This is a starting point for understanding what to review, not legal advice.** Talk to a lawyer or insurance professional for anything specific to your state or situation.

## Data you're likely handling

- Client name, address, phone, email
- Home/business access details (key locations, codes, alarm instructions)
- Payment information
- Employee or subcontractor personal information (if you run background checks)

## Areas to review

1. **Worker classification (employee vs. independent contractor)** — This is one of the biggest legal risk areas in the cleaning industry, especially for businesses that scale by adding subcontractors. Misclassifying an employee as a contractor can create real tax and legal exposure. The IRS publishes guidance on how this distinction is made (search "IRS independent contractor vs employee" at irs.gov), and some businesses request an official determination using IRS Form SS-8 if it's unclear.
2. **Background checks** — If you run background checks on employees or subcontractors (recommended, given the level of trust this business requires), using a consumer reporting service like Checkr means you're subject to the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), which the Federal Trade Commission enforces. This includes specific rules about getting consent and how to handle a negative result. See ftc.gov for FCRA compliance basics before running your first check.
3. **Chemical safety (Hazard Communication)** — If employees handle cleaning chemicals, OSHA's Hazard Communication Standard (29 CFR 1910.1200) requires that Safety Data Sheets (SDS) for those chemicals are accessible to employees and that containers are properly labeled. See osha.gov for the full standard and plain-language guidance.
4. **Liability insurance and bonding** — General liability insurance (covering accidental damage or injury) and a fidelity bond (covering theft) are standard for this industry and often expected by clients before they'll hand over a key. Ask a licensed insurance agent what's typical in your area.
5. **Business licensing** — Many cities/counties require a general business license, and some require a specific license for home-service or cleaning businesses. Check your city/county clerk's website or the Small Business Administration's local resources (sba.gov) for what applies where you operate.

## When to bring in a professional

- Before classifying anyone as an independent contractor rather than an employee
- Before running background checks on employees or contractors, to make sure your process follows FCRA requirements
- If a client alleges damage, theft, or an injury during a cleaning visit
